Triax is the cable that links a broadcast camera to the truck, and the connectors on the ends take most of the abuse. Sooner or later one needs replacing, and it's a job that is much easier to approach after watching someone who has done it before.

This video walks through replacing triax connectors on broadcast camera cable. It's part of the *Truck Guy Training with Sunny D* material on the n392ep channel.

## Why I recommend it

I've worked with the person behind this channel and consider him a very good broadcast resource. When someone with real truck experience takes the time to show a repair on camera, it's worth watching, whether you're doing the job yourself or just want to understand what's involved.

## Field notes

My own observations from rebuilding triax in the field.

### Connector prep

My main takeaway from this video is the method: use the actual connector parts themselves to set the cut lengths as you build the connector.

A lot of techs use a separate template or measuring box to get each strip length right. That works, but it takes more time. Using the connector components themselves as the reference is faster and more practical in the field.

The biggest caution is depth of cut. Don't score or strip so deep that you damage the shielding. Saving a little time on prep isn't worth compromising the cable before the connector is even assembled.

### Tools worth having for triax work

I use what crews usually call a "Trico" adapter to take the triax connection to BNC, so I can put a standard BNC TDR on the cable.

That gives me a better idea of where the fault is and which end I should attack first. It helps me avoid guessing, especially because triax rebuild kits are expensive and I'd rather not cut into the wrong end or sacrifice parts I didn't need to.

But the TDR isn't the final word. Sometimes the cable is ugly, sometimes the reading isn't perfect, and sometimes you just have to make it work. The final decision comes from the whole picture: the fault location, cable condition, previous repairs, continuity checks, and whether it actually works on a camera system.

### Checking the cable after the rebuild

Once the new connector is on, I check:

- Continuity
- Shorts between paths and layers
- The physical integrity of the connector and the repair
- A physical tug check
- Then I prove the cable on an actual camera/CCU system

A camera is still my ultimate triax tester. There's a lot I want to prove before I ever plug the cable into one, but the camera/CCU path is the final real-world check, because it runs the cable under real operating conditions.

### Rebuild or retire?

I don't use one single number to decide whether a triax cable gets rebuilt or retired. It's a judgment call based on the whole picture:

- Where the fault is
- How much usable cable is left
- Jacket condition
- Shielding condition
- Any sign of water getting in
- Previous repairs
- What the rebuild kit costs
- Whether the cable can still be made reliable enough for the job it's going to do
